Do the composer notes mean anything?

Every piece ships with a self-description its composer wrote in the same forward pass. These experiments measure whether that text carries real, piece-specific information: judges receive N blind score representations and N texts in scrambled order and must return the one-to-one pairing. Corpus: the free-form 30-samples-per-composer batches (11 composers × abc/codegen, 645 ok pieces1). Technical identifiers printed in the score header (key, tempo, meter, instruments, voice counts) are masked out of the texts, so matching has to run on musical content.2

Claims vs measurements

Structured claims were LLM-extracted from all 651 descriptions and checked against computed features. Descriptions are faithful to whatever the notation format could express, and silently wrong wherever the pipeline dropped the plan:

Self-recognition

Does a model recognize its own pieces? The 11×11 judge×composer matrix supports a difference-in-differences: a judge’s accuracy on its own puzzles, relative to its accuracy elsewhere, relative to how much every other judge drops on those same puzzles. This controls both judge ability and puzzle difficulty; the permutation test shuffles which judge plays “author”.

With full descriptions, self-recognition is real (+8.5pp, p = 0.006) and concentrates in the homogeneous-corpus composers — when all ten candidates sound alike, only author-side knowledge breaks ties. At summary granularity the effect vanishes (+0.3pp, p = 0.48): whatever privileged information a model has about its own pieces, one sentence can’t carry it.

Related results from the same corpus

Composers are identifiable from a single description embedding at 88%/85% (abc/codegen, chance 9%) — a stronger authorship signature than the audio itself (~42%). Showing the composer’s note to judges shifts scores by +0.15 on a 1–5 scale on average, but the shift correlates ρ = 0.64 with the intent-execution score: notes amplify faithful describers and penalize overclaimers.
